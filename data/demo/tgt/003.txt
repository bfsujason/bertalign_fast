“Timber—fall!”

With that loud, drawn-out shout, a towering larch—like a giant column of the Parthenon—came crashing down. Ye Wenjie felt the ground tremble beneath her feet. She picked up her axe and short saw and began trimming the branches from the massive trunk. At moments like this, she always felt as if she were preparing the body of a giant for burial. Often, she even imagined that the giant was her own father. The feeling she had two years ago on that tragic night, when she arranged her father’s body in the morgue, returned to her now. The split bark of the great tree seemed like the countless wounds covering her father’s body.

The six divisions and forty-one regiments of the Inner Mongolia Production and Construction Corps—more than a hundred thousand people—were scattered across this vast expanse of forests and grasslands. When they first arrived from the cities into this unfamiliar world, many of the educated youth carried a romantic expectation: when the Soviet revisionist empire’s tank columns crossed the Sino-Mongolian border, they would swiftly arm themselves and form, with their own flesh and blood, the first line of defense for the Republic. In fact, this had indeed been one of the strategic considerations behind the Corps’ formation. But the war they longed for was like the distant mountains on the horizon of the steppe—clearly visible, yet forever out of reach. And so they had only land reclamation, herding, and logging. These young people, who had once burned with passion during the “Great Networking,” soon discovered that compared to this vast world, even the largest inland cities were no more than sheep pens. In these boundless, frigid grasslands and forests, passion was meaningless: a surge of hot blood, once spilled, cooled faster than a pile of cow dung—and was less useful than the latter. Yet burning was their fate; they were a generation destined to burn.

And so, under their chainsaws and power saws, great oceans of forest were reduced to barren hills; under their tractors and combines, vast grasslands were plowed into cropland, and then turned into desert.

What Ye Wenjie witnessed in the logging could only be described as madness. Tall, straight Xing’an larches, evergreen Scots pines, slender white birches, sky-reaching aspens, Siberian firs, as well as black birch, Mongolian oak, elm, ash, willow—whatever they saw, they cut. Hundreds of chainsaws roared like a swarm of steel locusts; wherever her unit passed, nothing remained but fields of stumps.

The trimmed larch was about to be dragged away by a tracked tractor. At the other end of the trunk, Ye Wenjie gently stroked the freshly sawn surface. She often did this unconsciously, always feeling that it was a great wound, as if she could sense the tree’s intense pain. Suddenly, she noticed that not far away, on the sawn surface of another stump, there was also a hand gently stroking it. The tremor of feeling conveyed by that hand resonated with her own. Though the hand was fair, it clearly belonged to a man. Ye Wenjie looked up and saw that the person touching the stump was Bai Mulin, a thin, bespectacled young man. He was a reporter for the Corps’ Great Production News and had arrived at the unit just the day before yesterday to conduct interviews. Ye Wenjie had read his articles; his writing was excellent, carrying a kind of delicacy and sensitivity that felt utterly out of place in this rough environment—and was therefore unforgettable to her.
